An explainer of Neural Networks Are Already Clean by Nick Cammarata (Goodfire)

Eiffel Tower Qwen

"Neural Networks Are Already Clean" is a potentially ground-breaking AI interpretability paper by Nick Cammarata.
It claims that AI interpretability is potentially simpler than many in the field believed.
With Claude Opus 5.5 I tested key claims from the paper by building "Eiffel Tower Qwen", an homage to Golden Gate Claude, directly from the neurons of Qwen3-8B running on a prosumer graphics card.
This page seeks to explain, for lay readers, why this paper is so significant.

The paper

In Neural Networks Are Already Clean, Nick Cammarata of Goodfire looks at the individual "neurons" inside AI models. A neuron is one number the model calculates at each step as it reads and writes text. Qwen3-8B has 442,368 of them.

The dominant view in the field of AI interpretability has been that most neurons are a jumble of unrelated jobs or concepts (called superposition). Superposition meant that you couldn't identify one clear job/concept associated with a neuron. Instead a neuron might comprise a mix of totally unrelated concepts, making any further direct interpretation impossible without specialised tools.
This matters because it is the collective action of the neurons of an AI model that determine how it responds to human input. Change the neurons; change the output. If you could verifiably "read" a neuron then you could understand exactly why an AI came up with a given response.
The paper argues that (i) superposition is likely not correct and (ii) accordingly reading neurons is far easier than commonly understood.
To support those arguments, the paper asserts two key findings:

  1. Most neurons already do one readable job (rather than many). In Nick's audits of Qwen3-32B (a bigger version of the model we used), about 90% of neurons had one main job.
  2. Bigger ideas are built from those readable parts. A concept is a combination of a few understandable neurons, not something hidden in a tangle.

If those two findings are correct then AI interpretability is, potentially, a significantly more tractable issue than previously thought.
We believe we have independently reproduced the core of both findings in Qwen3-8B (the first only in part, see below), in a way anyone can check.
I have no background in ML or interpretability research, and relied heavily on Claude Opus 5.5 to run this experiment. We may be wrong, in aggregate or in the particulars, but we think our results are reproducible. This site aims to show that, and all the code is available open source on GitHub [link to come].

What we found

We used a small AI model called Qwen3-8B on a single RTX 5090. Claude Opus 5.5 wrote and ran the experiments; James set the questions and checked the results. In short:

  1. A handful of neurons carry each concept. Switch off 5 or fewer neurons and the model's odds of giving the right answer fall at least 20-fold. That held in 157 of 158 tests across 139 concepts. Switching off random neurons does nothing.
  2. Those neurons are readable. The Eiffel Tower runs through neurons for "landmarks", "buildings" and "Paris".
  3. You can steer with them. Turn them up and the model starts talking about the concept, much like Anthropic's Golden Gate Claude.
  4. You can do this directly from the neurons. Golden Gate Claude needed a sparse autoencoder to find its Golden Gate "feature". We needed no extra tooling: the neurons were already readable enough to find and use.

I don't claim these findings are unshakeable or perfect; only about 50% of the neurons we tested passed a strict one-job check, rather than the 90% the paper reports. Our "vibe-researched" approach no doubt has procedural issues or could be improved.
However, we do claim these results are reproducible, which we see as the key threshold.

The very fact an absolute novice (albeit with an extremely capable LLM assistant) could reproduce key findings from the paper on prosumer hardware within 24 hours is strongly corroborative of the central assertion(s) of the paper: that AI/neural net interpretability is more tractable than many previously thought.

What's new here

Golden Gate Claude (Anthropic, 2024) and the Eiffel Tower Llama (David Louapre, 2025) both steered with a sparse autoencoder: a second model, trained separately, that translates the AI's insides into readable features.
To test the paper's key claims we instead automatically traced which of the model's own neurons push it towards each concept, and then tweaked those neurons.
As the figures show, changing those neurons predictably changes the behaviour of Qwen3-8B without the need for a sparse autoencoder (or other) interpretation aid. That is the development the paper points to.

Steering with a few raw neurons isn't new in itself (Suau et al. 2022 did it with about 3), and we aren't claiming a new idea. What we add is a confirmation of the paper's central point, that this is tractable: on a small language model, on consumer hardware, in a day, by a non-specialist.

Built from shared parts

If concepts are built from readable parts, related concepts should share parts. We compared the top 50 neurons of 10 related pairs against 200 random pairs, after removing 18 general-purpose neurons that show up for almost everything.

Where in the model

The model processes text in 36 layers, one after another. Before running this test, we wrote down a prediction for where a concept's neurons would sit. It held: the concept's own neurons cluster early (recognising what's being asked) and in the middle-to-late layers (the concept's parts), with a shared set of "say the answer" neurons at the very end. This is not a novel discovery (see further reading), but it confirms that work and, the key point, it is directly traceable using the approach laid out in the paper.
It also illustrates a broader point: once individual neurons are readable, you can start to say what (some of) the layers of a particular model are doing.

How clean is the whole model?

We also checked 1,000 neurons picked at random to see how many have one clear job. With a strict check, about half did. The paper found about 90% in a model four times bigger. The details are in the methodology below.

What we haven't shown

This was a fast test, not a proof. The main gaps:

  1. Superposition. We didn't test it head-on, so we can't rule out neurons packing in unrelated jobs we didn't see.
  2. Needed isn't stored. Switching neurons off shows the model needs them. It doesn't show the fact lives there.
  3. One model. Everything here is Qwen3-8B. We didn't look at attention (the other main part of the model) at all.
  4. AI graders. Neuron descriptions come from GPT-6-Luna, checked by Jev. The steering scores we used to choose each concept's neurons come from a free local check that is a little generous. See the methodology.
  5. Steering is modest. On fresh questions the concept usually slips into the reply rather than taking it over, and people (Shakespeare, Mozart) are hard to steer fluently. Our (untested) hypothesis is that the behaviour in relation to certain famous people may be associated with post-training of the model to avoid impinging on copyrighted works.

How we did it

James read the paper and set the goal: could we reproduce Golden Gate Claude straight from neurons, and test the paper's claims along the way? Claude Opus 5.5 (running in Claude Code) wrote the code, ran the experiments overnight, logged every result and flagged its own mistakes. James reviewed and steered.

Methodology, for those who want the detail

Finding a concept's neurons

For each concept, an AI wrote 20 questions whose answer is the concept ("What is the name of the wrought-iron monument erected for the 1889 World's Fair in France's capital?"). We kept the questions Qwen answers correctly. For every neuron we then measured how much it pushes the model towards the answer: its activity multiplied by how much the answer's probability changes with it ("attribution").

Switching neurons off

We set the top neurons to zero and measured the model's chance of the right answer again, against the same number of random neurons from the same layers. "Odds fall 20-fold" means the answer's log-odds drop by 3; we use log-odds because a plain probability barely moves when the model starts out 99.8% sure.

Steering

We add a multiple of each neuron's strongest activity in ordinary web text, only while the model writes its reply (the question is read normally). Each concept's neurons (2 or 4) and strength were picked automatically from three simple rules, on a set of practice questions; Figure 3 uses 8 different questions. Replies are greedy: the model always picks its most likely next word.

Reading neurons

For all 442,368 neurons we stored the 40 passages of web text (from 10 million words) where each fires most strongly. GPT-6-Luna read them and described the neuron's job; Jev checked whether that description fits the passages, and also checked it against random text to catch descriptions vague enough to fit anything.

The census of 1,000 neurons

The describer alone says about 81% have one job. But it also says that about half the deliberately mixed "two-job" neurons we planted as a test, so it's too generous.

With a stricter check, which lets through only 8% of those planted mixtures, about 50% of positive ends and 47% of negative ends pass (each neuron has two "ends", strongly positive and strongly negative, judged separately). That's closer to a floor than an estimate.

So our number is somewhere between a half and four-fifths, on a model a quarter the size of the paper's. That's below 90%. We didn't test whether bigger models are cleaner.

Controls and checks

Random neurons from the same layers, for every switch-off and steering test. Three made-up concepts (a fictional bridge, mountain and linguist), which the model correctly fails to recall. Deliberately mixed neurons, to test the describer. Three tests were written down before we ran them (the first 20-concept run, the layer test and the census). For this page we re-ran every concept from scratch: the same top neurons came out each time.

Further reading

  • Golden Gate Claude, Anthropic (2024).
  • The Eiffel Tower Llama, David Louapre (2025). The same idea on Llama 3.1 8B, with a sparse autoencoder.
  • Suau, Zappella & Apostoloff (2022). Steering text with about 3 "expert" neurons.
  • Dai et al. (2022), "Knowledge Neurons", and Yona et al. (2026), single "entity cells", including in Qwen3-8B.
  • Geva et al. (2023), "Dissecting Recall": the three stages of fact recall.
  • Macocco et al. (2026): steering is weaker on chat-tuned models, Qwen3-8B included.
