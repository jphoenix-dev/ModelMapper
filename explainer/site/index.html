An explainer of Neural Networks Are Already Clean by Nick Cammarata (Goodfire)

Eiffel Tower Qwen

"Neural Networks Are Already Clean" is a potentially ground-breaking AI interpretability paper by Nick Cammarata.
It claims that AI Interpretability is potentially simpler than many in the field believed.
With Claude Opus 5.5 I confirmed key claims from the paper by building "Eiffel Tower Qwen"; an homage to Golden Gate Claude, via Qwen3-8B running on a prosumer graphics card, directly from the neurons.
This page seeks to explain, for lay readers, why this paper is so significant.

James Phoenix & Claude Opus 5.5 · October 2026 · tested on Qwen3-8B, an open model from Alibaba

The paper

In Neural Networks Are Already Clean, Nick Cammarata of Goodfire looks at the individual "neurons" inside AI models. A neuron is one number the model calculates at each step as it reads and writes text. Qwen3-8B has 442,368 of them.

The dominant view in the field of AI interpretability has been that most neurons are a jumble of unrelated jobs or concepts (called superposition). Superposition meant that you couldn't identify one clear job/concept associated with a neuron. Instead a neuron might comprise a mix of totally unrelated concepts, making any further direct interpretation impossible without specialised tools.
This matters because it is the collective action of the neurons of an AI model that determine how it responds to human input. Change the neurons; change the output. If you could verifiably "read" a neuron then you could understand exactly why an AI came up with a given response.
The paper argues that (i) superposition is likely not correct and (ii) accordingly reading neurons is far easier than commonly understood.
To support those arguments, the paper asserts two key findings:

  1. Most neurons already do one readable job (rather than many). In Nick's audits of Qwen3-32B (a bigger version of the model we used), about 90% of neurons had one main job.
  2. Bigger ideas are built from those readable parts. A concept is a combination of a few understandable neurons, not something hidden in a tangle.

If those two findings are correct then AI interpretability is, *potentially*, a significantly more tractable issue than previously thought. 
We believe we have independently confirmed both findings in Qwen3-8b in a provable way. 
I have no background in ML or interpretability research, and relied heavily on Claude Opus 5.5 to test this experiment. We may be wrong, in aggregate or in the particulars, but we think this is provable. This site aims to support that, and all the code is available open source on github [here].

What we found

We used a small AI model called Qwen3-8B on a single RTX 5090. Claude Opus 5.5 wrote and ran the experiments; James set the questions and checked the results. In short:

  1. A handful of neurons carry each concept. Switch off 5 or fewer neurons and the model's odds of giving the right answer fall at least 20-fold. That held in 157 of 158 tests across 139 concepts. Switching off random neurons does nothing.
  2. Those neurons are readable. The Eiffel Tower runs through neurons for "landmarks", "buildings" and "Paris".
  3. You can steer with them. Turn them up and the model starts talking about the concept, much like Anthropic's Golden Gate Claude.

However, we do claim these results are reproducible, which we see as the key threshold.

The very fact an absolute novice (albeit with an extremely capable LLM assistant) could reproduce key findings from the paper on prosumer hardware within 24 hours is strongly corroborative of the central assertion(s) of the paper; that AI/neural net interpretability is more tractable than anyone previously thought.

What's new here

Golden Gate Claude (Anthropic, 2024) and the Eiffel Tower Llama (David Louapre, 2025) both steered with a sparse autoencoder: a second model, trained separately, that translates the AI's insides into readable features.
To prove the paper's key claims we instead automatically surveyed the model's own neurons to find potential candidates where groups of neurons did one job/expressed one concept, and then tweaked those neurons to prove the principle. 
Per the figures, changing those neurons predictably changes the behaviour of Qwen3-8B *without the need for a further space autoencoder (or other) interpretation aid*. This is the key development of the paper. 

Steering with a few raw neurons isn't new in itself (Suau et al. 2022 did it with about 3). Built from shared parts

If concepts are built from readable parts, related concepts should share parts. We compared the top 50 neurons of 10 related pairs against 200 random pairs, after removing 18 general-purpose neurons that show up for almost everything.

Where in the model

The model processes text in 36 layers, one after another. Before running this test, we wrote down a prediction for where a concept's neurons would sit. It held: the concept's own neurons cluster early (recognising what's being asked) and in the middle-to-late layers (the concept's parts), with a shared set of "say the answer" neurons at the very end. This is not a novel discovery (see prior work), however it is confirmatory of that work and, the key development, explicitly and trivially traceable following the approach laid out in the paper. 
The ability to interpret the structure of the model in this way also reproduces a key assertion from the paper: understanding individual neurons makes it possible to extrapolate to understanding (some!) functions of the layers of those neurons in a particular model.

How clean is the whole model?

The paper's headline number is that about 90% of neurons have one job. We checked 1,000 random neurons. One AI read each neuron's strongest examples from web text and described its job; a second AI checked that description against the examples. Each neuron has two "ends" (strongly positive and strongly negative), judged separately.
[Comment: This needs simplifying or removing to a separate caveats section - it's not right for lay readers.]

  1. The describer alone says about 81% have one job. But it also says that about half the deliberately mixed "two-job" neurons we planted as a test, so it's too generous.
  2. With a stricter check, which lets through only 8% of those planted mixtures, about 50% of positive ends and 47% of negative ends pass. That's closer to a floor than an estimate.

So our number is somewhere between a half and four-fifths, on a model a quarter the size of the paper's. That's below 90%. We didn't test whether bigger models are cleaner.

What we haven't shown

This was a fast test, not a proof. The main gaps:

  1. Superposition. We didn't test it head-on, so we can't rule out neurons packing in unrelated jobs we didn't see.
  2. Needed isn't stored. Switching neurons off shows the model needs them. It doesn't show the fact lives there.
  3. One model. Everything here is Qwen3-8B. We didn't look at attention (the other main part of the model) at all.
  4. Mostly exploratory. Three tests were written down before we ran them: the first 20-concept run, the layer test and the census. The rest we designed as we went. [Comment: don't think we need to say this - its historiography of the process and we will do CR-3 to address it.]
  5. AI graders. Neuron descriptions come from GPT-6-Luna, checked by Jev. The steering scores in Figure 3 come from a free local check that is a little generous; we use it only to sort the menu. [Comment: we probably need a section on architecture/methodology that addresses how we actually did the checks for those who want to read it, and is collapsed by default + links to the gh repo.]
  6. Steering is modest. On fresh questions the concept usually slips into the reply rather than taking it over, and people (Shakespeare, Mozart) are hard to steer fluently. Our (untested) hypothesis is that the behaviour in relation to certain famous people may be associated with post-training of the model to avoid impinging on copyrighted works.

    Many of the hours went on finding faults in our own setup. Some examples:

    1. Our first Golden Gate prompts mentioned "orange towers", so the search found neurons for the word "orange".

    • Golden Gate Claude, Anthropic (2024).
    • The Eiffel Tower Llama, David Louapre (2025). The same idea on Llama 3.1 8B, with a sparse autoencoder.
