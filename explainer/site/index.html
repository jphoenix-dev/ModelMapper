An explainer of Neural Networks Are Already Clean by Nick Cammarata (Goodfire)

Eiffel Tower Qwen

A new paper argues that AI models are much easier to understand from the inside than most researchers thought. We tested its core claim in 24 hours on a home graphics card, with an AI doing most of the lab work. This page explains the paper, what we found and why it matters.

James Phoenix & Claude · October 2026 · tested on Qwen3-8B, an open model from Alibaba

The paper

In Neural Networks Are Already Clean, Nick Cammarata of Goodfire looks at the individual "neurons" inside AI models. A neuron here is one number the model calculates at each step as it reads and writes text. The model we used has 442,368 of them.

The usual view in the field is that most neurons are a jumble of unrelated jobs (called superposition), so you need extra tools to untangle them before you can read anything. The paper argues the opposite. It comes down to two things:

  1. Most neurons already do one readable job. In his audits of Qwen3-32B, a bigger version of the model we used, about 90% of neurons had one main job.
  2. Bigger ideas are built from those readable parts. A concept is a combination of a few understandable neurons, not something hidden in a tangle.

If that's right, you can read and edit a model neuron by neuron. That makes understanding how AI models work a much more tractable problem than we thought. We think the paper is one of the most important interpretability results in a while, so we wanted to test it.

What we found

We used Qwen3-8B, which runs on a single RTX 5090. Claude wrote and ran the experiments; James set the questions and checked the results. In short:

  1. A handful of neurons carry each concept. Switch off 5 or fewer and the model's odds of giving the right answer fall at least 20-fold. That held in 157 of 158 tests across 139 concepts. Switching off random neurons does nothing.
  2. Those neurons are readable. The Eiffel Tower runs through neurons for "landmarks", "buildings" and "Paris".
  3. You can steer with them. Turn them up and the model starts talking about the concept, much like Anthropic's Golden Gate Claude.

The takeaway: our results support the paper's core claim on a model anyone can run. They don't settle it. About half of neurons passed our strict one-job test, not 90% (the paper's model was 4 times bigger), and steering usually works only modestly. More on both below.

What's new here

Golden Gate Claude (Anthropic, 2024) and the Eiffel Tower Llama (David Louapre, 2025) both steered with a sparse autoencoder: a second model, trained separately, that translates the AI's insides into readable features. We used the model's own neurons, picked automatically, with nothing added. If the paper is right, that should work. It does, though more weakly than those polished demos.

Steering with a few raw neurons isn't new in itself (Suau et al. 2022 did it with about 3). What we add is the Golden Gate-style chat demo on a modern model, tested across 138 concepts.

Built from shared parts

If concepts are built from readable parts, related concepts should share parts. We compared the top 50 neurons of 10 related pairs against 200 random pairs, after removing 18 general-purpose neurons that show up for almost everything.

Where in the model

The model processes text in 36 layers, one after another. Before running this test, we wrote down a prediction for where a concept's neurons would sit. It held: the concept's own neurons cluster early (recognising what's being asked) and in the middle-to-late layers (the concept's parts), with a shared set of "say the answer" neurons at the very end. This matches earlier research on how models recall facts, so it's a confirmation rather than a discovery.

How clean is the whole model?

The paper's headline number is that about 90% of neurons have one job. We checked 1,000 random neurons. One AI read each neuron's strongest examples from web text and described its job; a second AI checked that description against the examples. Each neuron has two "ends" (strongly positive and strongly negative), judged separately.

  1. The describer alone says about 81% have one job. But it also says that about half the deliberately mixed "two-job" neurons we planted as a test, so it's too generous.
  2. With a stricter check, which lets through only 8% of those planted mixtures, about 50% of positive ends and 47% of negative ends pass. That's closer to a floor than an estimate.

So our number is somewhere between a half and four-fifths, on a model a quarter the size of the paper's. That's below 90%. We didn't test whether bigger models are cleaner.

What we haven't shown

This was a fast test, not a proof. The main gaps:

  1. Superposition. We didn't test it head-on, so we can't rule out neurons packing in unrelated jobs we didn't see.
  2. Needed isn't stored. Switching neurons off shows the model needs them. It doesn't show the fact lives there.
  3. One model. Everything here is Qwen3-8B. We didn't look at attention (the other main part of the model) at all.
  4. Mostly exploratory. Three tests were written down before we ran them: the first 20-concept run, the layer test and the census. The rest we designed as we went.
  5. AI graders. Neuron descriptions come from GPT-6-Luna, checked by Jev. The steering scores in Figure 3 come from a free local check that is a little generous; we use it only to sort the menu.
  6. Steering is modest. On fresh questions the concept usually slips into the reply rather than taking it over, and people (Shakespeare, Mozart) are hard to steer fluently.

How we did it

James read the paper and set the goal: could we reproduce Golden Gate Claude straight from neurons, and test the paper's claims along the way? Claude (running in Claude Code) wrote the code, ran the experiments overnight, logged every result and flagged its own mistakes. James reviewed and steered. The timeline:

    Many of the hours went on finding faults in our own setup. Some examples:

    1. Our first Golden Gate prompts mentioned "orange towers", so the search found neurons for the word "orange".
    2. An early measure saturates when the model is 99.8% sure, which made recall look spread across 20–50 neurons. On a better scale it's 1–5.
    3. Made-up control concepts (a fictional bridge in Ohio) caught one of our steps inventing recall that wasn't there.
    4. We expected the answer "rainbows"; the model says "Rainbow". Three concepts looked unrecalled until we let the model choose its own wording.

    Further reading

    • Neural Networks Are Already Clean, Nick Cammarata (Goodfire). The paper this page is about.
    • Golden Gate Claude, Anthropic (2024).
    • The Eiffel Tower Llama, David Louapre (2025). The same idea on Llama 3.1 8B, with a sparse autoencoder.
    • Suau, Zappella & Apostoloff (2022). Steering text with about 3 "expert" neurons.
    • Dai et al. (2022), "Knowledge Neurons", and Yona et al. (2026), single "entity cells", including in Qwen3-8B.
    • Geva et al. (2023), "Dissecting Recall": the three stages of fact recall.
    • Macocco et al. (2026): steering is weaker on chat-tuned models, Qwen3-8B included.
