Qwen Neuron Map

A map of every neuron in Qwen3-8B that we have tied to a job. Pick a concept and watch its neurons light up, layer by layer, as the model works out the answer.

James Phoenix & Claude Opus 5.5 · 18 October 2026 · work in progress

In Eiffel Tower Qwen we showed that a handful of neurons carry each concept the model knows: switch them off and it can't name the concept; turn them up and it talks about it. Since then we have run the same test on over 5,000 concepts. This page puts all of those results on one map.

A model like Qwen reads a question through a stack of layers, one after another. Qwen3-8B has 36 layers and 12,288 neurons in each. On the map, each row is a layer, with the question going in at the top and the answer coming out at the bottom. Each dot is one neuron that matters for at least one of the concepts we tested: about 44,000 of the model's 442,368.

What a neuron's "job" means here

We use three kinds of evidence. A neuron's job is clearest when they agree; for many neurons we only have one or two so far.

  1. What breaks without it. Switch the neuron off and see which answers get worse. This is the test behind every dot on the map, and it is the strongest evidence we have: it shows the model needs the neuron for that concept.
  2. What it pushes. When a neuron fires it nudges the model towards some tokens and away from others. We can read those tokens straight from the model's weights. This works well for neurons in the last third of the model, which write the answer, and poorly for earlier ones, which work through later neurons.
  3. What makes it fire. An AI (GPT-6-Luna) reads the web text where the neuron fires most strongly and describes the pattern; a second AI (Jev) checks that description against new text. We have done this for about 1,400 neurons so far.

Neurons fire positively or negatively, and the sign matters. Each neuron produces one number for every token, and it can be positive or negative. A positive number pushes the model one way; a negative one pushes it the exact opposite way. The two directions often do quite different jobs, so we describe them separately. Which side is called "positive" is an accident of how the model's weights are written down: flip the signs inside a neuron and the model behaves identically. So negative doesn't mean "anti"; what matters is which way the neuron pushes. About half of neurons happen to do their main job when firing negatively. The neuron that the African Union depends on pushes the model towards "African", "Africa" and "非洲" (Chinese for Africa) only when it fires negatively, which is how it fires when Qwen is asked about the African Union; its positive side pushes towards nothing in particular. When you hover over a neuron, the side it takes for the chosen concept is highlighted.

Names are for convenience. Where the evidence points clearly one way, a neuron gets a short name, like "Water and water supply". A name is a label to help you read the map, not the neuron's true meaning. We only show a name if it passes a blind check: Luna names the neuron without seeing one of the concepts that needs it, and a second AI, given only the name, has to pick that hidden concept out of five. Of the names Luna was confident about, 84% passed; a name taken from a different neuron passes about one time in eight, so a few names on the map may still be wrong. So far 1,827 neurons have a name that passed. That cost about $2 of AI time.

Many neurons have no name. We know they matter for the concepts listed, because the answer suffers without them, but we can't yet say what they do in a few words with confidence. That includes most neurons in the early layers. We show them anyway: knowing a neuron matters is a finding, even before we know why.

Kinds of job we have found

Each kind comes from a different test, so a neuron can have several, and most neurons have none we can name yet. Tap one in the figure ("Or see one kind of job") to see where those neurons sit.

    What the map shows so far

    1. Most of a concept's key neurons sit near the end. About two thirds of each concept's top five neurons are in the last eight layers, where the model writes its answer. A smaller cluster sits around layers 8–10, where the model takes in what the question is about.
    2. Neurons are shared. Almost half the neurons on the map matter for more than one concept. The "Shared" column holds the ones that serve several subjects at once.
    3. A concept's neurons feed each other. Switch off one of a concept's earlier key neurons and the later neuron that writes its answer fires about 12% less (the typical case), against almost nothing when we switch off a random neuron that was firing just as strongly. The arrows on the map show these links where we have measured them, so far for the top five neurons of 537 concepts; a run covering every concept's top 30 is under way.
    4. Facts are looked up in stages. Pick "Taj Mahal: which century?" in the figure. Neurons that recognise the Taj Mahal fire early, at its name; neurons that know the question is about a century sit later, and are shared with every other "which century" question; near the end come neurons for the answer itself, such as a 17th-century neuron, shared by everything built in that century.
    5. Some neurons hold facts, not names. A neuron in layer 28 fires for 18th-century people, places and works, whether you ask for a year, a century, a decade or a period, and switching it off makes the model worse at 18th-century dates. Next to it sit neurons that track how a date is written rather than when it was: one fires for years starting "17" but not for the words "18th century", and pushes the model towards "seven". Pick a century above to see them. These come from a separate, targeted test, so we call them "tested jobs" rather than names.
    6. A few neurons are two-ended scales. Most neurons that matter only do so in one direction. A few use both: firing one way pushes towards "north", the other way towards "south". We tested this two ways: whether each end's description, read from the weights, predicts which concepts use it, and whether pushing the neuron each way actually moves the model's answers towards that end (with a random neuron pushed the same amount as a control). Three pass both: two north/south neurons and a vertical/horizontal one. Two more probably do (breakfast/dinner, gentle/tough). A male/female first-names neuron moved the answers one way only. That is one job, a scale, not two: none of the 24 neurons we pushed showed two unrelated jobs at its two ends. Only gentle pushes give clean results; stronger pushes move even a random neuron's answers.
    7. The answer appears late. For a typical concept the right answer only reaches the top 10 at layer 33 of 36, and first place a layer later. Before that, the answer can't yet be read from the model's working state, even though many of the concept's neurons have already fired.

    What it doesn't show yet

    1. One task. Every dot comes from one kind of question: "what is this called?". A neuron can matter here and do other jobs elsewhere.
    2. A bias towards late layers. We find neurons by how much they affect the answer, which favours the layers that write it. Early neurons are under-counted.
    3. Attention, mostly. Each layer also has 32 attention heads, which move information between words. You can switch them on above, but we have measured far less about them.
    4. Jobs for every dot. Many dots so far only tell you which concepts need them. Neurons that matter for just one concept can't be named yet, because the blind check needs a second concept to hide.
